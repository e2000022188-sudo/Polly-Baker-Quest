Checkpoint 1 · Stage 1 — The Rub

The Legal Trap at Connecticut Court

Exhibit A · Indictment

Connecticut, 1747. The court calls Polly Baker before the bench. The charge: having children outside marriage, now for the fifth time. The magistrates present the case as a defense of public morality.

But the indictment has a missing half. The children have fathers. The legal and social burden falls on Polly while the men who helped create the situation escape the same punishment.

Case file: The court treats motherhood outside marriage as a public offense, yet the men responsible are not being prosecuted alongside Polly. The system's moral language conceals a gendered double standard.

Checkpoint 2 · Stage 2 — The Disguise

The Mask of Mock-Innocence

Exhibit B · Identity

Who is speaking? Polly Baker appears to be a poor, plain-spoken, pious woman addressing a court. But the voice is a literary disguise. Benjamin Franklin created the fictional speaker and presented the speech in a deadpan style that invites readers to take the performance seriously.

The mask

Polly Baker, the speaker

A seemingly uneducated woman uses the court's own moral and religious vocabulary. Her calm, earnest tone makes the argument more unsettling: she is not openly shouting at the judges; she reasons them into contradiction.

“May it please the Honourable Bench to indulge me in a few Words...”

Transcribed from the 1747 speech in the National Archives’ Founders Online edition. Franklin’s original capitalization is retained here.

The author behind the mask

Benjamin Franklin

Franklin's print hoax depends on a gap between the apparent speaker and the actual author. The speaker's apparent innocence is part of the satire, not proof that the text is a real court transcript.

Checkpoint 3 · Stage 3A — Set-Up / Trial of Wits #1

Scriptural Reverse-Logic

Exhibit C · Scripture

Polly does not reject the court's religious framework. She turns it around. If the community values the biblical command to “increase and multiply,” why is the woman who bears children treated as the criminal? Her argument makes the court's moral logic collide with itself.

Textual evidence · direct quotation
“Can it be a Crime (in the Nature of Things I mean) to add to the Number of the King’s Subjects, in a new Country that really wants People?”

Transcribed from the 1747 speech in the National Archives’ Founders Online edition. Franklin’s original capitalization is retained here.

Polly's bait: She frames childbearing as useful to a growing colony and invokes the religious command to multiply. This forces the judges to explain why conduct that produces new subjects deserves punishment.
The mark: A legal authority that claims to defend morality, but whose rules punish the woman while ignoring the fathers' responsibility.

Checkpoint 4 · Stage 3B — Set-Up / Trial of Wits #2

The Judicial Exposé

Exhibit D · Conflict

The argument becomes personal. Polly reveals that the man who first seduced and abandoned her has since become a magistrate. Crucially, she says she had hoped he would appear that day to moderate the court in her favor. The text does not say he was actually sitting on the bench during this hearing. The institutional hypocrisy remains: he has gained public authority while Polly bears the punishment for their shared history.

Checkpoint 6 · Stage 5 — The Getaway

The Transatlantic Hoax

Exhibit F · Archive

The final question is not only what Polly says, but how the speech travels. The National Archives' Founders Online edition identifies the earliest known printing as the London newspaper The General Advertiser on April 15, 1747. The speech spread quickly through other newspapers and magazines. Its fictional status was not widely understood: the text's authorship remained unknown until Franklin's confession was reported in 1788. The escape is the author's: the fictional speaker travels through print while readers mistake the performance for a real case.
